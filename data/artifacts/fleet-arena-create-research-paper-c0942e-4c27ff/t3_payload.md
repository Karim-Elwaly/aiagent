**Executive Summary (≈250 words)**  
Fusion research has entered a decisive acceleration phase between 2024 and 2026, with four reactor families—Magnetised‑Target Fusion (MTF), pulsed‑power tokamaks, inertial confinement fusion (ICF), and Z‑pinch—achieving milestones that bring them closer to commercial deployment. In 2024 the General Fusion LM26 device reached keV plasma temperatures during compression, demonstrating scalable MTF physics and reducing dependence on large external magnets【t2 line 5】. The Helion Polaris tokamak produced measurable deuterium–tritium fusion at 150 million °C in February 2026, validating a compact high‑temperature design that can be scaled to power plants with lower upfront cost【t2 line 6】. Pacific Fusion’s Sirius platform delivered over 3,000 stable shots by July 2026, proving the reliability and throughput required for grid‑connected operation【t2 line 7】. Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory’s NIF achieved repeatable breakeven in its 11th ignition experiment (7.9 MJ yield, gain ≈3.8) in June 2026, confirming laser‑driven ICF physics and opening a pathway to inertial fusion power plants【t2 line 8】. Zap Energy received DOE approval for a pre‑conceptual 50 MW net‑output Z‑pinch module using sheared‑flow stabilization in May 2026, offering a potentially lower‑cost route that can be engineered rapidly【t2 line 9】. Finally, General Atomics demonstrated a helicon radio‑wave current drive without a central solenoid in October 2026, simplifying tokamak construction and extending pulse length【t2 line 10】.  

Collectively these breakthroughs reduce capital costs, shorten engineering cycles, and demonstrate scalable physics across multiple reactor families. The convergence of private investment, federal support, and technical progress signals that the first generation of fusion power plants could emerge in the 2030–2035 window, provided continued policy incentives, focused research on materials and control systems, and integrated fuel‑cycle economics.
